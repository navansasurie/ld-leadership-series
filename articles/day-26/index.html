DAY 26 · INSTRUCTIONAL DESIGN & LEARNING EXPERIENCE DESIGN

Accessibility by Design: WCAG in Digital Learning

Is a course truly successful if some employees cannot operate or understand it?
Purpose & methodology

What this article will help you decide

Instructional design is not merely the production of content. It is the disciplined selection of experiences, evidence and performance support that help people execute important tasks reliably. This article demonstrates how to diagnose a design challenge, compare interventions and make an evidence-led decision.

Core approach: Embed accessibility acceptance criteria in storyboards, development and testing; map relevant requirements to WCAG 2.2.

Learning objective: By the end of this case, you should be able to articulate a defensible design choice, explain its trade-offs and identify what must be measured after deployment.

Illustrative fictional business case

CASE: Fairhaven Public Services

C — Context

A multilingual digital learning rollout has keyboard navigation gaps, low-contrast controls and inaccessible assessments.

Business outcome: Enable equitable completion and effective participation.

A — Analysis

A credible design decision begins with evidence. Interview practitioners and supervisors, inspect representative work outputs, review error patterns and observe high-stakes tasks. Separate capability gaps from process, technology and reinforcement barriers.

Hypotheses to test:

  • Accessibility is checked only at final QA
  • Custom interactions lack keyboard support
  • Alternative content is incomplete

These are working hypotheses, not established causes. A real engagement would require validation before committing budget.

S — Solutions and trade-offs

Compare three plausible choices rather than assuming the first training request is correct. Evaluate authentic performance, accessibility, production effort, maintainability and business risk.

OPTION A

Add a final accessibility checklist

Fastest apparent response, but may preserve the underlying performance gap.

OPTION B

Build accessibility into design and QA gates

Offers stronger alignment when paired with evidence and deliberate practice.

OPTION C

Offer a PDF as the only alternative

Can be appropriate under constraints but requires a clear rationale and controls.

E — Execution, governance and evidence

Executive recommendation: Prioritize keyboard operation, semantic controls, focus visibility, meaningful text alternatives and accessible assessment feedback; verify against applicable WCAG criteria.

  1. Discover: Agree on the operational outcome, baseline, target audience and critical tasks with the business owner.
  2. Design: Prototype the smallest representative learning experience and define acceptance criteria before scaling production.
  3. Validate: Test the design with representative learners, subject matter experts and relevant accessibility checks.
  4. Implement: Release with clear support ownership, feedback routes and manager reinforcement where needed.
  5. Evaluate: Compare performance with the baseline, investigate confounders and revise based on observed outcomes.

Primary measurement: Critical accessibility defects and successful task completion with assistive technology.

Key delivery risk: Automated scanners alone will miss usability barriers and interaction failures.

Decision gate: Do not scale until the pilot provides credible evidence that the selected design improves performance without unacceptable risk or maintenance cost.

Executive takeaway

The right learning experience is defined by the work people must perform, the evidence that demonstrates proficiency and the conditions under which transfer happens—not by screen count, media richness or course completion alone.
